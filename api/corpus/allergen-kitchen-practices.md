# Lantern Kitchen: Allergen and Kitchen Practices

This page describes how our kitchen works so that guests with allergies, intolerances and dietary rules can decide what is right for them. It describes practices, not individual dishes. The allergens contained in each dish as an ingredient are listed on the ordering menu, which our team keeps up to date and which is the source you should use to filter dishes. Nothing on this page should be read as a statement that a particular dish is free of any allergen.

## The most important rule: cross-contact is always possible

Lantern Kitchen is a busy, compact kitchen with one wood-fired oven, one wok station, one grill, one fryer area and a cold section for salads, tartare and desserts. We handle all of the major allergens every day: gluten, dairy, egg, peanut, tree nuts, soy, fish, shellfish, sesame, mustard, celery and sulphites. Although we clean surfaces, change utensils and follow written procedures, we cannot promise that a dish is completely free from traces of an allergen. The allergens listed on the menu are the ones that are deliberate ingredients. They do not include traces from shared equipment, airborne flour, splashes or hands.

If you have a severe allergy, such as anaphylaxis, we encourage you to speak with a member of the team before ordering, even when you have used the filters on your phone. The chef on duty can tell you whether it is possible to prepare a dish with extra precautions, and can also tell you honestly when it is not. Where we are not certain, we will say so and we will not guess. Guests with severe allergies should carry their own medication at all times.

## How we handle an allergy order

When you tell your server about an allergy, the server writes the allergen on the order and carries it to the kitchen pass. The sous chef or chef on duty reads the ticket aloud, checks the ingredients of each dish against our recipe book, and confirms with the server whether the dish can be made. A dish for an allergy is prepared at a cleaned station, with a clean pan or board, fresh gloves and clean utensils. It is plated by the same cook who prepared it and is carried to the table by the server who took the order, not left on the pass for anyone to collect. The server states the allergen aloud when the plate is placed so that you can confirm it.

If you add a note to your order through the QR menu, for example "no nuts", the note is displayed on the kitchen screen. However, the kitchen screen is a convenience and not an allergy system: because notes can be missed in a busy service, the allergy procedure above is only triggered when a person tells a server. Please always speak to a member of staff in addition to using the app.

Our assistant on the ordering page answers questions using the same menu data. When asked about allergens, it uses the structured allergen information, and if it is unsure it will say so and offer to call a waiter. It does not decide that something is safe.

## Shared fryers and the gluten-free fryer

We have two fryers. The main fryer is shared by several battered or breaded dishes, so oil in it carries gluten, egg, fish and shellfish. The menu mentions the shared fryer in the description of each of the dishes that use it. We also have a smaller fryer that is reserved for gluten-free items only, the shoestring fries. It has its own baskets, its own scoop and its own oil, which is filtered every day and changed every three days. Nothing battered or breaded is ever put into the gluten-free fryer, and the baskets are stored separately and are a different colour. Even so, the gluten-free fryer sits next to the main fryer and the two can be affected by splashing in a very busy service. If you have coeliac disease, you may wish to ask the chef about this on the day.

Fryer oil is a blend of sunflower and rapeseed oil. We do not use peanut oil in any fryer.

## Woks and sesame oil

The wok station uses a high-heat neutral oil for cooking and finishes some dishes with a splash of toasted sesame oil, soy sauce and other sauces. Sesame, soy, fish sauce and shellfish products are all used at the wok station. Woks are wiped and heated between orders and are scrubbed and washed at the end of each service, but they are not washed between every single dish. For an allergy order, we use a separate, freshly washed wok, and the cook is asked to wash their hands before they start. Sesame is present in several forms in the kitchen, including toasted seeds, tahini, sesame oil and black sesame crumble, so guests with a sesame allergy should talk to a member of staff.

## Gluten-free preparation

Gluten-free dishes are prepared in a kitchen where wheat flour is used heavily. Flour is airborne around the wood-fired oven and pizza bench, so the cold station and the wok are positioned away from that corner. For gluten-free orders the cooks use a clean board, a clean knife and a separate colander and pasta pot. Gluten-free crackers are stored in a sealed container. Soy sauce used in cooking contains wheat unless a dish is noted as gluten free, in which case a wheat-free tamari is used for that dish. Beer is used in some batters, and we use oat milk, which contains gluten in the form that we buy it, for the oat milk latte.

We do not claim that our kitchen is a gluten-free kitchen. People with coeliac disease may be able to eat many of our gluten-free dishes, but each person must make their own decision after speaking with the chef.

## Nut handling: peanuts and tree nuts

Peanuts and tree nuts are used in this kitchen. We store them in sealed, labelled containers on a dedicated shelf that is below nothing else, so that a spill cannot fall onto other food. Peanut sauce is made in small batches in a dedicated pot using its own spoon, and crushed roasted peanuts are kept in their own tub and applied with a dedicated scoop. Pine nuts, almonds and pistachios are used in other dishes and are handled in the same way. Peanuts are legumes and tree nuts are a separate allergen group, so the menu lists them separately and the filter treats them separately. A guest who is allergic to peanuts is not necessarily allergic to tree nuts, and vice versa, but many people are allergic to both, so we encourage you to say exactly what you react to.

Coconut, nutmeg and pumpkin seeds are not treated as tree nuts by our kitchen, although individual guests sometimes react to them. If you are concerned, please ask. We do not offer a nut-free guarantee for the restaurant as a whole. Dishes that are described on the menu as containing nuts are made with nuts as ingredients, and dishes that are not described that way are not made with nuts as an ingredient, but they may be made in the same room.

## Dairy, egg and soy

Butter, cream and cheese are used in many dishes, and butter is sometimes added at the end of cooking in the pan, so a dish that appears to be dairy free should be checked by the chef if the guest has a severe milk allergy. Dairy-free dishes on the menu are those with no dairy ingredient, and the dairy free tag refers to ingredients only. Egg is used in batters, aioli, noodles, desserts and as a topping. Soy appears in soy sauce, miso, tofu, edamame and some dessert ingredients. Oat milk and coconut milk are our two non-dairy milks, and no plant milk is certified free from cross-contact.

## Fish, shellfish, mustard, celery and sulphites

Fish sauce, shrimp paste and shellfish stocks are used in some savoury dishes and in house ferments, and these ingredients are not always obvious from a dish's name. If you avoid fish or shellfish for allergy or religious reasons, check the allergen list rather than assuming from the dish description. Mustard appears in dressings, pickles and sauces. Celery appears in stocks and in celery stock used to cook rice and risotto, as well as in salt blends. Sulphites occur in wine, vinegars, dried fruit and some preserved products and are listed when they are present at a significant level. Guests who react to sulphites should ask about wine, since the only wine we pour by the glass contains them.

## Vegetarian and vegan: what the words mean here

We use the vegetarian tag for dishes that contain no meat, poultry, fish, shellfish or animal-derived stock. Cheeses are made with microbial or plant-based rennet so that vegetarian dishes with cheese are vegetarian in the strict sense; where a menu description says a cheese is "vegetarian-friendly", this is what is meant.

We use the vegan tag for dishes that contain no animal products at all: no meat, fish, dairy, egg, honey or gelatine. Vegan dishes are cooked in the same kitchen as meat and fish dishes, with the same woks, ovens and grills, though we use clean pans and utensils for guests who ask for a strictly vegan preparation. We cannot promise that the cooking surfaces have never touched an animal product. Some guests are happy with that, and others are not, so we leave the decision to you. Sugar used in our desserts is not bone-char filtered and wine is not guaranteed to be vegan unless the menu says so.

## Halal sourcing

Dishes carrying the halal tag are made with meat from halal-certified suppliers and are cooked without alcohol. This applies to the meat itself, to stocks and to marinades. Not every meat dish on the menu is tagged halal. Pork, bacon and non-certified beef are cooked in this kitchen, on a separate flat-top and with their own tongs, boards and knives, and are stored in labelled containers in their own part of the walk-in fridge. Nevertheless, we are not a halal-certified restaurant, and the halal tag describes the sourcing of the ingredients and the avoidance of alcohol in the dish, not the status of the whole kitchen. Guests who require a fully certified kitchen should choose their dishes accordingly or talk to the manager.

## Alcohol in cooking

Alcohol is used sparingly in cooking, and only in dishes that are not tagged halal. Beer is used in some batters. Where a dish contains wine, beer or spirits, the menu description says so. Alcohol is largely, but not completely, cooked off, and we cannot give a precise residual figure. Guests who avoid alcohol should speak to staff.

## Suppliers, labels and changes to recipes

Our menu data are checked against supplier labels every time a supplier changes a product, and at least once a month in any case. Packaged ingredients such as sauces, crackers and stocks are the most common source of unexpected allergens, so we keep every label on file. We tell the kitchen team about changes at the start of each shift. Nonetheless, supply problems happen. If a regular ingredient is unavailable and a substitute is used, the substitution is discussed at the pre-service briefing and the menu is changed, or the dish is taken off the menu. The menu data available to the ordering app are updated by the manager on duty. If you see a difference between the menu in your hand and what the server tells you, trust the server and ask them to check with the chef.

## Staff training

Everyone in the front and back of house completes allergy awareness training before their first service, and refreshes it every year. The training covers the major allergens, how to take an allergy order, how to answer honestly when we do not know, and what to do in an emergency. At least one person on each shift holds a current first-aid certificate. In an emergency we call the local emergency number first, and we will help a guest use their own prescribed medication if they ask us to. We do not stock emergency medication.

## What to do if you are not sure

If you are at all unsure whether a dish is right for you, ask. You can ask your server, who will check with the chef; you can press the call-waiter button on the ordering page; or you can ask the assistant, which will point you to the menu information and can call a waiter. We would much rather answer ten questions than have you take a risk. Where we cannot be certain, our policy is simple: we say so, and we recommend a simpler dish or a conversation with the chef. We never tell a guest that something is safe unless the chef has checked it for their particular allergy that day.

## How the menu filters work

The ordering menu lets you exclude allergens and require dietary tags. Excluding an allergen hides every dish whose ingredient list includes it, and requiring a tag shows only dishes that carry it. These filters are applied against the structured data for each dish, which is maintained by the kitchen, and not against the wording of the dish description. For that reason a dish that mentions a nut in its description but has no nut ingredient listed would still be hidden only if the allergen is on its list, and the team reviews every description against the data when the menu changes. A filter cannot see cross-contact, so a dish that survives your filter has no deliberate ingredient from the excluded group, nothing more. If a dish is temporarily unavailable it is hidden from the menu regardless of filters.

## Cross-contact on the table and in the dining room

Allergens can also travel on the table. Servers carry plates on trays, and where a tray holds several dishes, a guest with an allergy has their plate carried separately. Shared sauces, such as peanut sauce or aioli that arrives in a small pot, are served in a pot dedicated to that dish and not decanted into another. Condiment bottles on the table are wiped between guests. When food is shared across the table, an allergic guest should use their own serving spoon. Children's plates and half portions are prepared in the same way as full portions and follow the same allergy procedure.

## Intolerances and preferences

Not every dietary need is an allergy. Guests who avoid onion and garlic, who are low-FODMAP, who follow a religious or ethical diet not covered by our tags, or who simply dislike an ingredient can all ask for changes. Because most dishes are prepared to order, the chef can often leave out a garnish, a sauce or a topping. Changes that remove an ingredient are usually easy, while changes that replace the base of a dish, such as noodles, rice or bread, may not be possible. We tell you straight away when a request cannot be met. A modified dish does not make the dish safe for an allergy, and any allergy should be reported as an allergy and not as a preference.
