# About Lantern Kitchen: Our Story, People and Suppliers

## How Lantern Kitchen began

Lantern Kitchen opened in a former chandlery on Harbour Street in the spring of 2019. The founders, chef Mei Lin Tan and her brother Daniel Tan, grew up above their parents' noodle shop and wanted to build a restaurant that felt as relaxed as a family kitchen but cooked with the care of a much more formal one. The name comes from the paper lanterns that hung over the family shop, and from the idea of a warm light left on for anyone passing by. The first menu had twelve dishes and eight tables. Today there are forty-two dishes, twelve tables in the main dining room, a patio and a private room, but the idea has not changed: a short menu cooked properly, in a room where nobody feels rushed.

The building still has its original brick walls and a long timber beam that used to carry the chandlery's hoist. The wood-fired oven in the corner was installed in 2021, built by a mason from Naples over eleven days and fired for the first time on a rainy Thursday with a menu of only three pizzas. It has run almost every day since.

## The kitchen team

Chef Mei Lin Tan leads the kitchen. She trained in Singapore and in a Mediterranean restaurant on the coast of Spain, and the menu reflects both: wok-fired noodles and charcoal-grilled skewers sit beside burrata, risotto and roast chicken. She writes every recipe herself and tests every new dish on staff at the family meal before it is allowed on the menu. Sous chef Oluwaseun Adeyemi runs the pass during service and is responsible for the allergy procedure described on our practices page. The pastry section is run by Priya Nair, who makes all of the desserts, sorbets and ice creams in-house from small batches.

Daniel Tan manages the front of house and the ordering systems. He is the person to contact about bookings, events and feedback, and he reads every email sent to hello@lanternkitchen.example. The team is small, about twenty-two people across all shifts, and many have been with us for years. We hold a family meal before every service where cooks and servers eat together and talk through the night ahead, including the special requests and bookings.

## Sourcing: where our ingredients come from

We buy from a small number of suppliers so that we can know them by name. Vegetables and herbs come from Ridgeline Farm, forty minutes inland, delivered on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings. In summer the tomatoes, basil and squash arrive on the same day they are picked. The farm also grows the Thai basil and the chillies that go into our curries and sauces, and in the colder months we use their stored root vegetables and squash.

Our fish is delivered fresh each morning by Tideline Seafoods, a family-run fishmonger at the docks. The cod, salmon and squid come in whole or as large fillets and are cut in our kitchen, which is why portions are generous. The tuna for the tartare is sushi-grade and is bought only in small quantities, twice a week, so it is always fresh. Prawns are bought frozen from a supplier that audits its farms, because fresh prawns are rarely available in our region.

Meat comes from Hollis Brothers, a butcher who supplies free-range chicken and grass-fed beef. Our halal-certified chicken and lamb come from a separate halal-certified supplier, Al-Noor Meats, and are delivered separately, stored separately and labelled. The pork belly is bought from Hollis Brothers and delivered in sealed trays. Dairy comes from a creamery in the next county. The mozzarella, burrata and parmesan are bought from importers who supply cheese made with microbial rennet.

Rice, noodles, spices, coconut milk, tamarind and sauces are bought from an importer in the city, who brings us goods from Thailand, India and Japan. The turmeric, cumin and cardamom are bought whole and ground in-house each week. Our wine is a single Pinot Grigio from a family vineyard in north-eastern Italy, chosen because it is crisp and works with the menu.

## Sustainability

We try to waste as little as we can. Vegetable trimmings go into stocks, and chicken carcasses go into the broth. Bread and pizza offcuts are dried and made into crumb. Whatever is left is collected twice a week by a composting service that returns finished compost to Ridgeline Farm, so the soil that grows our vegetables is fed in part by our scraps. Fryer oil is collected by a company that turns it into biodiesel. Takeaway containers and cutlery are compostable, and we have not used single-use plastic straws since 2020. The wood for the oven comes from a local tree surgeon, and the ash goes back to the compost.

Our menu is seasonal at the edges. The core dishes stay the same all year, but side dishes, soups and desserts change when the ingredients do, and the QR menu and the dish guide are updated on the same day. We measure food waste each week and post the figure on the staff noticeboard; our target is under four per cent of food purchased by weight.

## Community

We run a Monday staff meal that is open to local residents who are alone or on a low income, with a pay-what-you-can bowl of the day. Once a month we host a cooking evening in the Lantern Room for children aged eight to twelve, led by Priya, to teach them to make dumplings and ice cream. We pay all staff at least the local living wage, and we share gratuities across the team in the way described in our policies. Every year in October, Lantern Kitchen donates a day of takings to the local food bank.

## Awards and press

Lantern Kitchen has never chased awards, but we are proud of a few. In 2022 the regional food guide named our Pad Thai the best noodle dish in the harbour district, and in 2023 the local newspaper's readers voted us the neighbourhood restaurant most worth a detour. We hold the city's highest food-hygiene rating, which is displayed on the front door, and we were inspected most recently in the spring. We do not pay for reviews and we do not offer free meals in exchange for them.

## Visiting the kitchen

Guests are sometimes curious about the wood-fired oven and the wok station, and when service is quiet we are happy to show you. Ask your server in the early evening on a weekday and one of the cooks will take you to the pass, explain how the oven reaches its temperature and let you see the dough that has been fermenting for two days. We do not offer tours during busy periods or for large groups, because a crowded kitchen is a dangerous kitchen. Schools and cooking clubs can arrange a visit on a Monday morning by emailing the front-of-house manager, and we give each visitor a short tasting of whatever the pastry section is testing that week.
